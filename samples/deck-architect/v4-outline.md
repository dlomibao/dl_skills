# Four moves that turn a skill into a system

## Audience Model
- **Who's in the room:** Claude Code power users who have authored at least one production skill of their own and can read SKILL.md frontmatter without flinching. Hardest sell: the maintainer of a ten-skill catalogue who believes nothing in a talk about two third-party skills can possibly apply to theirs. Second-hardest: the minimalist who treats every ban list as bloat. `[INFERRED — confirm]`
- **What they care about:** triggering reliability (does the skill actually fire when relevant?) and leverage (when it fires, does the output change in a way they can measure?). Context cost is a secondary worry they surface when pushed. `[INFERRED — confirm]`
- **What they already know/believe:** how Claude loads skills, that the `description` field drives invocation, that long SKILL.md files chew context. They believe their own skills are fine. They do not yet distinguish instructing from enforcing. `[INFERRED — confirm]`
- **Transformation (walk-in → walk-out):** "My skills are fine; this is a book review of someone else's" → "I know four moves, I know which two to apply first, and I have a 72-hour way to check whether I made things worse."
- **Likely objections / deep-dive triggers:**
  1. "Why these two skills and not Anthropic's first-party library?"
  2. "Twelve rows of rationalization-rebuttal pairs per skill — my context is toast."
  3. "Enforcement is obvious for a visual skill. What does it look like for a testing skill?"
  4. "Where is the actual evidence this works?"
  5. "If my enforcement pass tanks triggering, how fast do I find out?"

## Brief
- **Decision/ask:** before Friday, pick one skill you own, apply two of the four moves in this deck, and wire the 72-hour invocation-count check.
- **Format & time:** 12-minute live talk plus about five minutes of questions. Technical-conference register.
- **Constraints:** ten main slides plus backup. Self-contained HTML deliverable. No corporate template.
- **One-sentence takeaway:** **A great skill is an enforcement system tuned to Claude's specific failure modes — not an instruction manual for behaviors you wish the model had.**
- **Team contributors:** N/A — single-author analysis of two third-party skills.

## Narrative spine
**SCQA + Pyramid (Minto).** The audience does not want a guided tour of two skills; they want the answer on slide 1 and MECE evidence behind it. Four moves, four supporting slides, honest costs, rollback, ask. Walking through "here is skill A, here is skill B" would lose the room before the STAR.

## Cover slide
- **Title:** *Four moves that turn a skill into a system.*
- **Subtitle (≤12 words):** *Read from impeccable and superpowers, for authors of other skills.*
- **Meta:** *Design-lessons talk · 12 min*

One `<h1>`, one subtitle, one neutral meta row. No kicker eyebrow, no second display block, no continuation-word fragment. Reads as one deck name in one breath.

## Opening hook (first 30 seconds)
*"Your skills tell Claude what to do. The two most-installed community skills spend most of their bytes telling it what not to do — and one of them ships a node script that audits twenty-four anti-patterns with no model in the loop. That is the gap between a skill you wrote for yourself and a skill people install."*

Spoken, not displayed. The hook and STAR are the same artifact: the idea that the best community skills are enforcement systems dressed as capability skills, concretized by a runtime-free CLI shipping inside a so-called "design skill." No separate manufactured peak.

## STAR moment
Slide 2, the reframe: both skills look like capability additions and are in fact discipline-enforcement systems wrapped around the model's known defaults. That sentence is what the audience quotes in the hallway afterward.

Disarm the expectation of a tool tour — promise instead that nothing in the next eight slides depends on installing either skill.
   - Load: balanced
   - Visual: text-only

2. **Both skills are enforcement systems dressed as capability skills.**

3. **Vague priors lose. Name the specific default you want killed.**
   - Purpose: the first move, with proof from both skills.
   - On-slide: side-by-side. Left — impeccable's font reject list, eleven named families. Right — superpowers' "1% chance it applies means you MUST check" from the frontmatter of the using-superpowers skill.

"Do not use Inter, DM Sans, Plus Jakarta, Instrument Serif, Fraunces, Space Grotesk, Newsreader, Lora, Crimson, Outfit, Cormorant" forces a decision. Generalizes outside visuals: deck-architect ships a forbidden-phrase list for the same reason. Name the token you want killed; name the replacement.
   - Load: balanced
   - Visual: text-only

4. **Rebut the rationalization before the model writes it.**
   - Purpose: the move that separates usable from installed.
   - On-slide: a five-row excerpt from the Red Flags table — left column *what the model is about to think*, right column *the rebuttal that fires first*. Rows: "this case is special" / "no exceptions — tests still run"; "the user is in a hurry" / "speed comes from skipping review, not skipping tests"; "this is a trivial change" / "trivial changes are where silent regressions hide"; "I already understand the problem" / "write the failing test and confirm your understanding"; "one-percent risk is acceptable" / "one-percent means you MUST check".
   - Speaker notes: the excuses Claude reaches for under time pressure are uniform across sessions. Observe the ones your own skill provokes once, then write the rebuttal inline. This one move is why superpowers feels different from "a good prompt" — the rationalization is named before it runs.
   - Load: slide-heavy
   - Visual: text-only stylized table

8. **What you give up: longer files, sharper edges, real authoring time.**
   - Purpose: the tradeoff slide. A deck without one reads as a sales pitch.
   - On-slide: three honest costs named in parallel — SKILL.md roughly doubles when you add a ban list and a rebuttal table; opinionated skills fight the user on edge cases, and the user needs to be able to override; you cannot write a ban list for a domain you have not lived in, so the first pass on any skill is weak and gets sharper only with invocation data.
   - Speaker notes: the smallest useful version is one banned token plus one rebutted excuse. That is twenty authoring minutes, not a weekend. Address the "sounds heavy" instinct directly — the audience is thinking it before you say it. Name the skill you are not going to ban-list this quarter and why.
   - Load: balanced
   - Visual: text-only

9. **If this backfires, catch it in 72 hours — not next sprint.**
   - Purpose: rollback in main flow. This is a decision deck asking for a change to a working artifact.
   - On-slide: three rows. (a) Keep the prior SKILL.md next to the new one as `SKILL.md.prev` — one-line diff is your rollback. (b) Log invocation count for 72 hours after the change. (c) If invocations drop by more than 30 percent against the trailing week, triggering regressed — restore, diagnose, retry. Plus three "when not to do this" constraints — no enforcement on skills with fewer than five real invocations; no ban without a specific replacement; if enforcement consistently fights user intent, the user wins.
   - Speaker notes: anticipate the "I will not notice regressions for a week" objection. The 72-hour check is cheap, survives being forgotten, and catches the failure mode of a ban list with no replacement.
   - Load: slide-heavy
   - Visual: text-only

10. **One skill. Two moves. Friday.**
    - Purpose: close on the takeaway plus the specific ask, full screen.
    - On-slide: the takeaway sentence in the largest type, then the action — *Pick one skill. Apply Move 1 and Move 4. Ship by Friday.* — then the 72-hour check, reduced to one line.
    - Speaker notes: the deck succeeds when the audience names a skill. Ask for one aloud before the room moves on. Move 1 and Move 4 are the "if unsure" pair — Move 1 is the cheapest, Move 4 is the check that catches the failure mode of Move 1.
    - Load: balanced
    - Visual: text-only

## What changed and why (v4 vs v3)
- **Slide 7 is now an SVG flow, not seven text paragraphs.** v3 wrote the seven skill names as a labeled list because the old renderer had no structured spec to draw from — text-first won by default. v4 emits a `type: flow` YAML spec in the `Visual:` field; the HTML renderer embeds it as `<figure data-visual-spec>`, and `scripts/render-visual.js` post-processes it to inline SVG inheriting the deck's accent, ink, paper, and typography tokens.
- **Slide 5 added as a quadrant.** The "enforcement + breadth" framing existed in v3 only as speaker notes; giving it a 2×2 anchors it spatially and gives the audience a place to plot their own skills during the talk.
- **Slide 6 bar chart moved from backup to main flow.** v3 buried Mejba's 14% / 23% data in B3. The bar chart is the strongest available evidence; promoting it to main is the honest move, especially because slide 7 claims the chain effect explains where the savings came from. The bar sits on the slide that earns it.
- **B2 graph and B3 waterfall added as structured specs.** B2 was text-only in v3; B3 is new. Both auto-earn visuals under 2.5.0's topology rule (≥3 nodes, directional structure).
- **Takeaway rewritten.** v3 was "The best skills aren't instructions — they are enforcement systems tuned to Claude's specific failure modes." v4 keeps the spirit but tightens to "enforcement system tuned to failure modes" as a nominal phrase, which reads better on slide 10 at display size.
- **Cover title and subtitle rewritten.** v3 was "The design moves behind impeccable and superpowers." v4 is "Four moves that turn a skill into a system." — the takeaway idea ("system") lives in the cover itself, and the number four primes the four-slide defense.
